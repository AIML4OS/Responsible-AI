Module 1. Specifics of Responsible AI in Official Statistics

Responsible AI for Official Statistics

1 Introduction

Artificial Intelligence (AI) and Machine Learning (ML) create new opportunities for official statistics. They can support data processing, automation of repetitive tasks, missing value imputation, information classification, alternative data analysis, and the preparation of result descriptions.

At the same time, the use of these technologies entails new challenges. A result generated by a model may be erroneous, incomplete, biased, or difficult to explain. Data provided to an external tool may be subject to unwanted disclosure. Automation can also make it difficult to determine who is responsible for a decision and how the result was produced.

In official statistics, the consequences of such problems extend beyond a single analytical error. They can affect the quality of published data, the interpretation of social and economic phenomena, and public trust in official statistics.

Therefore, responsible use of AI should not be understood solely as compliance with IT security rules. It encompasses the entire process: from defining the objective of application, through the selection of data and methods, to validation, interpretation, dissemination of results, and monitoring of the solution.

The module uses as key reference points materials from the United Nations Economic Commission for Europe (UNECE), in particular the Responsible AI for Official Statistics Framework (2025), Generative AI for Official Statistics (2025), the publication Ethics in Official Statistics (2026), and the UN Fundamental Principles of Official Statistics.

2 Module Objectives

After reviewing this material, the participant should:

  • understand why the responsible use of AI is of special importance in official statistics;
  • distinguish technological benefits from actual value for the statistical process;
  • recognize fundamental risks related to quality, confidentiality, transparency, and accountability;
  • understand why legal compliance does not always exhaust ethical issues;
  • be able to identify basic prerequisites that should be met before deploying an AI solution.

3 1.1. What is Responsible Artificial Intelligence?

Responsible Artificial Intelligence (Responsible AI) is a human-centered, value-driven, and holistic approach to designing, developing, deploying, and using AI systems. It focuses not so much on the technical capabilities and features of the models themselves, but on our own role and responsibility for the impact of their operation on people, organizations, and society as a whole.

In practice, this means that evaluating a solution cannot be limited to the question:

Does the model work?

The following questions must also be answered:

  • Does the model solve the right problem?
  • Does it use appropriate data?
  • Are its results sufficiently reliable?
  • Can errors have a disproportionate impact on selected groups or areas?
  • Can the way the model is used and the limitations of its results be explained?
  • Is the data processing method secure and compliant with applicable requirements?
  • Has a person or entity been designated as responsible for overseeing the solution?
  • Can the organization detect a problem, respond to it, and decommission the model if necessary?

Accountability should accompany the entire lifecycle of the solution. It is not a one-time check performed after software development is complete.

3.1 Accountability vs. Technical Correctness

Three levels of evaluation for a solution should be distinguished:

Level Example Question
Technical Correctness Does the code execute without error?
Methodological Correctness Is the applied method appropriate for the objective, and have the results been properly verified?
Responsible Use Is the application justified, secure, transparent, and subject to appropriate oversight?

A positive evaluation at one level does not guarantee a positive evaluation at the others.

For example, a model may generate results with low prediction error but systematically overestimate values for a specific group of units. Code may run correctly but remove a portion of observations in a way that contradicts study assumptions. Conversely, a tool may be technically secure, but its application may lack sufficient methodological justification.

Conclusion: trust in official statistics is built on adherence to professional standards, scientific integrity, and ethical considerations. Implementing responsible AI requires rigorous and transparent methodologies that guarantee quality at every stage: data collection, processing, storage, and presentation.

4 1.2. Why Does Official Statistics Require Special Caution?

Official statistics provides information used by public administration, businesses, researchers, media, and citizens. Statistical results can influence economic assessments, public service planning, demographic analysis, and social and economic decision-making.

The credibility of statistics relies on trust, not just on calculations alone. For data to be recognized as official and reliable, a statistical office must be guided by professional independence, impartiality, data confidentiality, transparency of methods used, and full protection of respondent privacy. These guidelines define the UN Fundamental Principles of Official Statistics.

Artificial intelligence must support these principles, not undermine them. A strong reputation is the most important capital of any statistical institution. Any breach of trust (e.g., through the use of unverified or opaque AI models) carries two severe risks:

  • Refusal to provide data: if citizens or companies lose confidence in the protection of their information, they will stop participating in surveys and sharing data.

  • Loss of status as an authoritative source of knowledge: if users consider AI-generated results erroneous or non-transparent, the statistical office will lose its standing as a trusted authority.

4.1 Key Features of Official Statistics

4.1.1 1. Public Trust

Users must have a justified conviction that statistics are compiled based on sound methods and that the way they are prepared does not unduly favor specific interests.

The use of AI should not make it harder to demonstrate that the statistical production process remains under the institution control.

4.1.2 2. Statistical Quality

High algorithmic efficacy is not synonymous with high statistical quality. Depending on the application, considerations must include, among others:

  • accuracy and reliability of results;
  • consistency with the adopted methodology;
  • comparability over time and space;
  • timeliness;
  • completeness;
  • uncertainty of results;
  • impact of the method applied on statistical indicators and aggregates.

4.1.3 3. Confidentiality

Microdata collected for statistical purposes require special protection. The use of AI does not remove the obligation to maintain confidentiality, nor does it justify automatically transferring data to any arbitrary tool.

4.1.4 4. Impartiality and Professional Independence

Statistical methods and procedures should be selected based on professional and scientific criteria.

An AI solution should not be adopted simply because it is trendy, vendor-recommended, or organizationally expected. Its usefulness should be evaluated relative to a specific statistical problem.

4.1.5 5. Transparency and Accountability

The organization should be able to explain how AI was used, what data and methods were applied, how result quality was evaluated, and who is responsible for process oversight.

The scope of documentation and explanation should be tailored to the nature and risk of the given application.

5 1.3. AI in Public Administration vs. AI in Statistical Production

Not every application of AI in an office constitutes an application of AI in statistical production. This distinction is essential because the purpose of using the tool determines how its quality and risk are evaluated.

Area of Application Example Key Control Question
Administrative support Preparing a draft letter or meeting summary Is the content accurate, complete, and verified?
Programming support Generating R code for data cleaning Does the code preserve analysis assumptions and pass tests?
Statistical data processing Automatic classification of activity descriptions Is the classification sufficiently accurate and consistent?
Statistical methods Missing value imputation Does the method improve result quality and preserve appropriate statistical properties?
Estimation and forecasting Forecasting tourist numbers Is the model superior to proper baseline methods and are its errors acceptable?
Statistical communication Generating narrative descriptions for publications Does the description faithfully reflect the data without misleading the reader?

Different purposes for AI use require distinct approaches to risk assessment:

  • Administrative applications (e.g., draft writing or document summarization) primarily involve the risk of disclosing confidential or internal information. They should not be assumed to be automatically safe.

  • Applications in statistical production require special methodological scrutiny. Generative AI is non-deterministic, as its outputs may vary with each query, and any error propagates to subsequent process stages, affecting results shared with a wide audience.

Control standards for AI solutions:

Good practice: before selecting a tool, determine whether AI will support an employee, automate a technical task, or directly affect statistical values or their interpretation.

Bad practice: applying a single control procedure to all AI applications, regardless of data type, purpose, and the scale of error consequences.

6 1.4. Responsible AI, Ethics, and Law

Legal compliance is a necessary component of responsible AI use, but it does not always cover all ethical issues.

The UNECE publication Ethics in Official Statistics (2026) emphasizes the importance of ethics in an evolving data ecosystem and the need to distinguish between what is legally permissible and what is ethically responsible.

The mere fact that a specific action is not prohibited by regulation does not mean it constitutes ethical and sound statistical practice. This stems from the fact that legal frameworks often lag behind the rapid pace of technological development.

Even if a given AI application is fully legal, the following must be evaluated before deployment:

Justification and Purpose Is the use of AI genuinely justified and needed in this process?
Proportionality Is the scale of applied measures and potential risk proportional to the benefits?
Impact on Public Trust Will the solution refrain from undermining institutional credibility in the eyes of citizens and data users?

6.1 Example: Publicly Available Data

Suppose a team wants to use an AI model to link information from multiple publicly available sources.

The mere fact that information is publicly available does not automatically mean that combining it in any way is justified. The following must be checked:

  • whether the data may be used for the given purpose;
  • whether combining sources increases the risk of re-identifying individual units;
  • whether the resulting dataset is of adequate quality;
  • whether the data use is proportional to the objective;
  • whether the method and its limitations are properly documented.

6.2 Example: Automated Draft Comments on Results

A generative model prepares a description of the growth in the number of enterprises based on a statistical table.

Even if the model uses aggregated data exclusively, it may attribute changes to the wrong cause, confuse reference periods, or exaggerate the significance of a minor difference.

Responsible use therefore requires verifying not only the consistency of the text with the table, but also whether the narrative goes beyond what is legitimately supported by the data.

Conclusion: evaluating an AI application should include at least three questions:

  1. Is the action compliant with applicable regulations and institutional rules?
  2. Is it methodologically justified?
  3. Are its consequences acceptable from the perspective of professional ethics and public trust?

7 1.5. Benefits and Risks of AI Use

AI can support the modernization of official statistics, but benefits do not materialize automatically. They depend on data quality, method selection, staff competencies, and how the solution is deployed and monitored.

Situation Potential Benefit Condition for Responsible Use
Imputation of missing values Faster data preparation Validation of method and impact on statistical results
R code generation Shorter development time Testing, code review, and output verification
Text classification Automated coding Error evaluation and control of ambiguous cases
Documentation assistant Easier access to knowledge Reliable sources, response verification, and access controls
Narrative generation Faster report drafting Verification of description accuracy against data

Time savings alone should not be the primary criterion. It must be assessed whether AI delivers real value without an unacceptable decline in process quality, security, or credibility.

7.1 Potential Benefits

  • Efficiency: reduction of time-consuming, repetitive tasks.
  • Scalability: ability to process large datasets and numerous documents.
  • Analytical support: identification of patterns and anomalies that would be difficult to detect manually.
  • New data sources: supporting the analysis of text data and other alternative sources.
  • Better documentation: supporting the creation of method descriptions, code, and user guides.
  • Information accessibility: facilitating search and explanation of statistical publication content.

7.2 Key Risks

  • Errors and hallucinations: generation of false information or faulty code.
  • Bias: uneven result quality across different groups of units.
  • Loss of confidentiality: improper data transmission or disclosure of confidential information.
  • Lack of transparency: difficulty explaining how results were produced and inability to fully trace the model reasoning path.
  • Over-automation: reduction of expert oversight.
  • Vendor lock-in: difficulties in reproducing the process or switching tools.
  • Quality degradation over time: deterioration of model performance following changes in data or conditions.

8 1.6. Examples of Good and Bad Practices

Below are several sample scenarios designed to illustrate practical challenges associated with deploying artificial intelligence in the work of an analyst and statistician. These situations are purely educational and illustrative; they do not represent actual incidents that occurred in statistical offices.

8.1 Case A: Code Generation for Data Cleaning

Situation

An analyst uses a generative model to prepare R code that standardizes column names, removes duplicates, and joins data from several files.

Bad practice

The analyst copies the code into a production script because it executes without error. They do not verify whether the post-processing record count matches expectations or whether information was lost during column joins.

Good practice

The analyst:

  1. Tests the code on a small, controlled dataset.
  2. Checks record counts and data structure before and after processing.
  3. Verifies how missing values, duplicates, and conflicting values are handled.
  4. Compares results with an independently prepared control example.
  5. Documents significant changes and maintains process reproducibility.

Conclusion

AI can speed up programming, but it does not replace testing or responsibility for data processing logic.

8.2 Case B: Forecasting Model for Statistical Results

Situation

A team builds a model to forecast the number of tourists staying in accommodation establishments.

Bad practice

The model achieves low training error. The team considers it superior and deploys it without comparison to a baseline method, without temporal validation, and without evaluating regional performance.

Good practice

The team:

  1. Compares the model against reasonable baseline methods.
  2. Evaluates performance on out-of-sample data unused for fitting.
  3. Applies a split that respects observational chronology.
  4. Analyzes errors across key statistical breakdowns.
  5. Checks result stability across different periods.
  6. Defines guidelines for monitoring and re-evaluating the model.

Conclusion

Model quality should be evaluated under conditions corresponding to actual future deployment, not solely on training data.

8.3 Case C: AI Documentation Assistant

Situation

An organizational unit creates an assistant to answer questions about procedures, methodology, and guidelines.

Bad practice

The assistant answers questions without citing sources. If the AI model does not provide sources, the employee cannot tell whether the answer is based on official office guidelines or invented by the algorithm from general web content.

Good practice

The solution uses vetted documents, cites answer sources, flags when information is insufficient, and is tested on ambiguous questions to ensure it cannot be easily misled.

Conclusion

Faster workflows carry no value if bought at the risk of error. An AI assistant is valuable only when a human can rapidly and independently verify the truthfulness of the provided answer.

8.4 Case D: Microdata Processing

Situation

An employee wants to accelerate microdata analysis by sending a slice of a dataset to a publicly available AI tool.

Bad practice

The employee assumes that limiting the record count is sufficient to ensure security.

Good practice

The employee uses an approved environment, checks the permissibility of the planned processing, minimizes data scope, and consults uncertainties with data protection and security officers.

Conclusion

Security depends on data type, processing method, and tool usage terms, not merely on dataset size.

9 1.7. How to Assess Whether AI Use is Justified?

Before launching a project, it is worth conducting an initial assessment covering six areas.

Area Question
Objective What specific problem is AI intended to solve?
Alternatives Does a simpler method solve the problem equally well?
Data Are data of sufficient quality and permitted for this purpose?
Quality How will result accuracy be verified?
Risk What harm or issues could occur in the event of an error?
Oversight Who approves results and responds to anomalies?

If these questions cannot be answered, it does not automatically mean the project should be rejected. It means that before deployment, analysis must be supplemented, tests planned, or the scope of application restricted.

9.1 Principle of Proportionality

Deploying artificial intelligence does not require creating complex and lengthy audit procedures for every single tool, even the simplest. The key to efficient institutional operation is the principle of proportionality, which dictates that the scope and rigor of control must match the actual risk level of a given project.

Risk profile difference:

  • Low risk: if AI helps correct typos or formats headings in an internal memo, an algorithmic error is easy to spot and immediately fix by a human. This application poses no threat to published data quality.

  • High risk: if a model calculates inputs directly affecting official statistics, an undetected error could lead to flawed economic decisions and loss of institutional trust.

To determine the required detail of verification for a given AI system, 6 key factors should be analyzed:

  • type and sensitivity of data;
  • impact of the solution on statistical results;
  • error detectability;
  • scale and reversibility of consequences;
  • degree of automation;
  • feasibility of independent verification.

Conclusion: the principle of proportionality does not mean waiving controls in simpler cases. It means adapting them to actual risks. Simple auxiliary tools require only quick employee verification, whereas complex systems impacting statistical production require full methodological validation, security audits, and formal sign-off. With this approach, the office avoids stifling innovation with bureaucracy while effectively safeguarding data quality and security.

10 1.8. Exercise: Is This Responsible AI?

10.1 Scenario Description

A statistical team is preparing a tool to automatically generate short descriptions of summary tables. The tool uses a language model. An employee inputs a table, and the model generates text describing the key changes.

During the pilot phase, it was found that:

  • the tool significantly reduces the time required to draft the initial description;
  • in most cases, it correctly identifies the direction of changes;
  • it occasionally confuses the reference period;
  • in some responses, it attributes observed changes to causes that cannot be inferred from the table;
  • there is no formally defined procedure for reviewing text prior to publication.

10.2 Tasks for Participant

  1. Identify at least three benefits of using the tool.
  2. Identify at least four risks.
  3. Determine which risks could directly impact the credibility of published statistics.
  4. Propose a minimum set of controls prior to deployment.
  5. Decide whether the tool can be deployed in its current form.
Proposed Solution

Potential benefits:

  • shorter drafting time;
  • standardization of initial descriptions;
  • reduction of repetitive tasks.

Key risks:

  • confusing reference periods;
  • misinterpreting the direction or scale of change;
  • attributing unjustified causes;
  • lack of uniform content oversight;
  • risk of publishing an erroneous description.

Recommended controls:

  1. Automated checks of reference periods, units, and numerical values.
  2. Restricting generation of causes unbacked by data and approved sources.
  3. Testing on a set of correct, ambiguous, and difficult test cases.
  4. Mandatory substantive verification prior to publication.
  5. Error logging and regular quality evaluation.

Decision: in its current form, the tool should not independently prepare texts for publication without human review. The pilot may continue, or the tool may be allowed as a drafting assistant, provided appropriate safeguards are implemented.

12 1.10. Key Takeaways

  1. AI is a means to an end, not an end in itself. Its use should have a specific rationale, solve a concrete problem, and deliver tangible value.
  2. Technical correctness does not guarantee statistical correctness. Code and models always require independent substantive human verification.
  3. Quality must be evaluated in the context of the application. A low average model error may conceal significant issues in specific breakdowns.
  4. Data confidentiality and security remain fundamental obligations. New technologies do not waive existing requirements.
  5. Accountability does not end at deployment. Documentation, monitoring, and corrective capabilities are necessary if the model begins producing errors.
  6. Public trust is a critical evaluation criterion. AI should enhance the credibility of official statistics, not undermine it.
  7. Control should be proportional to risk. Editorial tools require different safeguards than models directly affecting statistical results.

Core theme of the module:

Responsible AI in official statistics means the conscious and controlled use of technology to support the quality, credibility, and utility of statistics, while maintaining confidentiality, transparency, and professional accountability.
